### How I Study DFIR Every Day — My Routine, Tools, and Mindset


![image](/images/articles/how-i-study-dfir/img0.jpg)


New month, new blog.

One question I get a lot — especially from people trying to break into DFIR — is _“How do you actually study this stuff?”_ Not just what courses to take, but **how you structure it** , **what tools you use** , and **how you stay consistent without burning out**.

This post is my honest breakdown: my daily DFIR routine, the platforms and subscriptions I use, what I’m planning to add next, and why showing up daily matters more than fancy certs or marathon study sessions.

### Disclaimer (lol)

Before I get into anything else, I want to be clear about one thing:

I’m **not** a DFIR analyst **(yet).**

I’m a **security analyst** who **works incident response** , and I’m actively building toward the long-term goal of becoming an **incident response consultant**. This blog isn’t written from a place of mastery, it’s more written from the middle of the journey.

And honestly, that’s intentional.

There’s a lot of polished content out there from people who are already deep into DFIR. What I don’t see as often is someone openly sharing **how they’re learning** , **how they structure their days** , and **how they stay consistent while still figuring things out**. That’s what this post is about.

This is how I study DFIR daily, the platforms and tools I rely on, what I plan to add next, and why consistency (not perfection) is what’s moving me forward.

### Why DFIR Pulled Me In

DFIR (Digital Forensics & Incident Response) clicked with me the first few months I started studying Cybersecurity because it felt like the one way I could feel fulfilled solving real problems in real time. You’re not just learning theory — you’re figuring out _what happened_ , _how it happened_ , and _how to contain it_.

DFIR brings together:

  * Forensics
  * Memory analysis
  * Malware behavior
  * Logs (lots of logs…..)
  * Network traffic
  * Threat intel
  * Detection engineering



It’s messy. It’s complex. And that’s what makes it good.

### Why Studying DFIR Daily Matters

I learned (the hard way) that DFIR is not something you can cram.

This field:

  * Has **too many moving parts**
  * Changes constantly
  * Requires pattern recognition and instincts
  * **Is heavily muscle-memory based**



If you don’t touch logs or artifacts for weeks, things fade fast. Daily exposure — even short sessions — compounds **fast**.

Daily study helps with:

  * Faster triage during investigations
  * Recognizing attacker behavior earlier
  * Staying current with TTPs
  * Building intuition instead of just knowledge



Consistency beats intensity every single time.

### My Daily DFIR Study Routine

This routine sort of changes over time. Some days are longer, some days are lighter — **but I aim to touch DFIR** **every day**.

### Morning (30–60 minutes): Exposure & Awareness

This is about staying plugged in.

  * Reading threat intel write-ups (The DFIR Report, Huntress blogs, etc.)
  * Skimming recent incidents and campaigns
  * Reviewing new IOCs or attacker techniques
  * Looking at a Sigma rule or detection logic
  * Light reading — no pressure, just context



Even this alone keeps your DFIR brain “on”.

### Main Study Block (1–2 hours): Hands-On Practice

This is where real learning happens.

What I typically do:

  * Work through a DFIR or SOC lab in HackTheBox, TryHackMe, CyberDefenders, etc.
  * Analyze one dataset (logs, memory, PCAP, disk artifacts)
  * Practice triaging data
  * Recreate an attack flow mentally or on paper



Some days it’s memory forensics, other days it’s Windows event logs or ELK detections.

But the key is **touching real data consistently**.

### Evening / Wind-Down (15–20 minutes): Passive Learning

Short, low-pressure learning.

  * Watch a DFIR or malware analysis video
  * Listen to a talk or breakdown
  * Take quick notes
  * Add key takeaways to my notes system



This keeps momentum without mental overload.

### Advice for Anyone Starting DFIR

If you’re just getting into DFIR:

  * Start with **logs** , not malware
  * Learn Windows internals early
  * Write things down in your own words
  * Study different data sources (endpoint, network, cloud)
  * Don’t **just** chase tools — chase understanding
  * Be consistent, even if it’s just 30 minutes each day (or even 5 minutes!)



Repetition builds confidence. Confidence builds skill.

### Final Thoughts

DFIR is not learned overnight.

It’s built:

  * One log at a time
  * One investigation at a time
  * One routine at a time



Daily effort compounds faster than you think. This routine will change. The tools will change. The threats will change.

What won’t change is the value of showing up.
